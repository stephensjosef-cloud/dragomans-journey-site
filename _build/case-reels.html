The program

CLIENT_LONG runs an early childhood center, and one of its programs teaches kids as young as three to cook with whatever is in season. Many recipes come with garden and science activities, so the kids learn where their food comes from while they stir.

The program's creator films each class on her phone.

The pile

A single class produces 16 to 20 clips, anywhere from ten seconds to a couple of minutes each. Somewhere in there is a great 60-second reel, buried under cleanup shots, empty pots and a lot of waiting for things to bake.

Cutting that down by hand is an evening of work for every episode. On a small nonprofit team, that evening usually doesn't exist.

What I built

The Reels Engine. The clips go in along with the recipe card. A motion scan keeps the action and drops the cleanup and empty-pot shots. I read a contact sheet of what's left and write the edit, and the engine renders a vertical 1080×1920 reel with the program's own title-card art.

Reels follow the order things were filmed, and the captions follow what's actually on screen, so a reel always matches the class that happened.

The house rules

Every note the program's creator gives becomes a rule the engine checks on every reel after. A few of them:

  • Captions sit centered in rounded boxes and wrap so no word is ever stranded alone on a line.
  • Whenever something cooks on screen, the temperature and time go in the caption.
  • Reels run 45 to 90 seconds, and simpler recipes get shorter reels.
  • Clips flow straight into each other, with no hand pulling away after adding an ingredient.

The gate

The program's creator approves every reel before it posts. Nothing has gone out without her yes, and nothing will.

What's next

Seven more episodes, filmed in October, are going through the engine now. Alongside the reels, I'm building the program its own app: a seasonal cooking app for teachers and families, on iPhone, iPad and Android.
